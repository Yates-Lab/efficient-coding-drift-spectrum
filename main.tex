Goal is to understand the statistical structure of images formed on moving sensors (such as animal eyes) and understand it's implications on retinal coding. Deriving a complete statistical description of images formed in moving optical systems is difficult. We make the simplifying assumption that the statistics of the movement changes in stationary increments and independent of the scene being imaged and analyze pair-wise statistics.
Under these assumptions, we can derive an analytical form of the autocorrelation function $C_z(\xi,\tau)$, which can then be studied in the Fourier domain. 
The power spectrum is generally not separable in spatial and temporal frequencies. 
We study the structure of the power spectrum arising from power-law natural images undergoing Brownian motion.
Assuming well-known statistical structure of natural images, the resulting power spectrum has interesting properties. 
Specifically, slicing at a spatial or temporal frequency, power is flat up to a cut-off frequency, and then falls as a function of $f^{-4}$ or $\omega^{-2}$. 
Derive efficient coding models that predict optimal filters that are center-surround. 

Statistical structure of drifting natural images (either under empirically observed drifting eye motion or Brownian) has been reported before (Rucci ....). Here, we seek describe the statistical structure of the data, and understand its implications for retinal coding. 

\section{Correlation structure of moving images}
% \afbcomment{Consider writing the signal as $I(x,t) = I_0 (x - u(t))$.}

For clarity, we present the derivations in one spatial dimension. The extension to two spatial dimensions is straightforward. Let $s(x)$ denote a static image, where $x$ denotes position. The time-varying displacement $u(t)$ produces the movie
\begin{align}
    z(x,t) = s\big(x-u(t)\big).
\end{align}
We assume that $u(t)$ has stationary displacement increments and is independent of the static image $s(x)$. 
Under the assumptions of $u(t)$ above, the probability density of displacements is a function of spacial displacement $\xi$ and temporal lag $\tau$, given by
\begin{align}
    u(t+\tau) - u(t) \sim \rho(\xi,\tau).
\end{align}
The space-time autocorrelation $C_z(\xi,\tau)$ can be written as a simple spatial convolution of the spacial autocorrelation function $C_s(\xi)$ and the movement density function $\rho(\xi,\tau)$,
\begin{align}
    C_z(\xi,\tau) &= \big\langle z(x, t) \, z(x+\xi,t+\tau)\big\rangle_{x,t} \\
        &= \big(\rho(\cdot,\tau) * C_s\big)(\xi)
\end{align}
The derivation is given in Appendix~\ref{sec:app_autocorrelation}. Since the spatial convolution becomes multiplication in the Fourier domain, the space-time power spectrum is
\begin{align}
    C_z(k,\omega) =  \rho(k,\omega)\, C_s(k).
\end{align}

To describe fixational eye drift, we specialize the motion model to Brownian motion.

Let $u(t)$ evolve stochastically from initial position $x_0$ via Brownian motion $W_t$ with diffusion constant $D$:
\begin{align}
    du(t) = \sqrt{2D} dW(t),\quad u(0) = x_0.
\end{align}
The probability distribution of the SDE solution is $\rho(\xi,\tau)$, where $\xi$ and $\tau$ is space and time, respectively
\begin{align}
    \rho(\xi,\tau) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi D|\tau|}} \exp\!   \left( -\frac{\xi^2}{4D|\tau|} \right).
\end{align}
The space-time fourier transform of $\rho(\xi,\tau)$ has a simple form (see appendix~\ref{sec:app_drift} for derivation details). 
A well known property of static natual images is that the power spectrum is power-law in spatial frequency. This means that $C_s(k) \propto k^{-\beta}$ for some $\beta>0$. 
For Brownian drift, the stimulus power spectrum depends on the diffusion constant $D$. We make this dependence explicit by writing $C_z(k,\omega;D)$. The resulting spectrum is given by the static-image power spectrum $C_s(k)$ multiplied by the space-time Fourier transform of $\rho$:
\begin{align}
    C_z(k,\omega;D) & = \rho(k,\omega)\, C_s(k) \\
    % &= \frac{2Dk^2}{(Dk^2)^2+\omega^2}\,C_s(k) \\
    & = \frac{2Dk^{2-\beta}}{(Dk^2)^2+\omega^2} \label{eqn:drift}
\end{align}
for $D>0$. 

 

For the natural-image exponent $\beta=2$, the spatial-frequency dependence in the numerator of Eq.~\ref{eqn:drift} cancels. At a fixed nonzero temporal frequency, the spectrum is approximately flat below the spatial crossover $k=\sqrt{|\omega|/D}$ and falls as $k^{-4}$ above it. Likewise, at fixed spatial frequency, the temporal spectrum is approximately flat below $|\omega|=Dk^2$ and falls as $\omega^{-2}$ above it. Figure~\ref{fig:spectra-betas} shows this partial whitening and the dependence of the spectrum on the static-image exponent and diffusion constant.

The fall of the power spectrum differs from previous reports of natural-movie statistics (Dong and Atick, Jun et al., ...). Dong and Atick analyzed camera footage and movie scenes and found a nonseparable power spectrum with power-law decay in spatial and temporal frequency. World-fixed camera footage has also been described by a separable spectrum, $C(k,\omega)\propto k^{-2}\omega^{-1.3}$ (Jun et al.). The drift model instead predicts a crossover that couples spatial and temporal frequency.

Brownian drift already flattens the stimulus spectrum over part of the spatiotemporal frequency range. This raises a question for efficient coding: if eye movements reduce spatial correlations before neural processing, why should subsequent processing still favor center--surround filters?

\section{Efficient coding of drifting images}
Brownian drift flattens the stimulus spectrum over part of the spatiotemporal frequency range. This raises a question for efficient coding: if eye movements already reduce spatial correlations, why should subsequent neural processing favor center–surround receptive fields? To address this question, we optimize a linear encoding model for the statistics of drifting images.

To address this question, we model the neural response using a linear space-time filter $w$. The response $r(x,t)$ of the linear model is a function of the input $z(x,t)$ and noise $n_{in}(x,t)$ and output noise $n_{out}(x,t)$,
\begin{align}
    y(x,t) = \big(w*(z+n_{in})\big)(x,t), \quad r(x,t)=y(x,t)+n_{out}(x,t).
\end{align}
Here, $*$ denotes convolution over space and time. We take the input and output noise to be independnt Gaussian noises with zero mean, with variance $\sigma_{in}^2$ and $\sigma_{out}^2$, respectively. We seek to find the filter $w$ and drift constant $D$ that maximizes the mutual information between the input $z$ and response $r$, subject to a power and temporal derivative constraint on the response.  

Constraints on the filter are necessary because, without them, the filter gain can be trivially increased to surpass the output noise~\cite{linsker1992local}. To prevent this, we constrain the output variance of the filter, similar to prior work~\cite{linsker1992deriving,atick1992does}. We introduce an additional constraint on the temporal derivative of the outputs as high frequency responses to not come for free in neural systems, they require larger neurons and increased metabolic cost. This constrainst is also applied to filter outputs before output noise is added. The information maximization objective together with the variance and smootheness constraints results in the follwing  optimization problem
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \underset{w,D}{\text{maximize}} \quad & I(Z;R) \\[4pt]
        \text{subject to} \quad & \left\langle y(x,t)^2 \right\rangle_{x,t} + \eta \left\langle \dot{y}(x,t)^2 \right\rangle_{x,t} = 1.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\eta\ge 0$ controls the strength of the temporal smoothness penalty. The procedure for solving this optimization problem is described in full in Appendix~\ref{sec:app_optimal_filters}. In brief, we express the objective and constraint in the Fourier domain and introduce a Lagrange multiplier. In doing so, we obtain a closed-form expression for the optimal filter power at each fixed diffusion constant $D$. We determine the multiplier numerically so that the combined variance and smoothness constraint is satisfied. We then find the optimal diffusion constant $D^*$ by searching over $D$, reoptimizing the filter at every trial value.

We first build intuition using static natural images, then examine the optimal filter for drifting images at a fixed diffusion constant, and finally allow diffusion to adapt as the noise levels change. The effect of the temporal penalty on the optimal diffusion constant is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:diffusion-eta}; we hold $\eta$ fixed for the noise comparisons below.

\subsection{Center--surround filters despite partial whitening}

Before applying this model to drifting images, we consider the illustrative example of the whitening or decorrleting theory of the retina for the case of static natural images~\cite{atick1992does,olshausen1997sparse,simoncelli2001natural}. Its well known that the spatial power spectrum of natural images is approximately $C_s(k)\propto k^{-2}$~\cite{field1987relations}. A filter with power gain $|w(k)|^2\propto k^2$ would flatten, or whiten, the signal spectrum, since the filtered signal has power $C_s(k)|w(k)|^2$. With input noise and a limited response-power budget, however, amplifying weak high-frequency components also amplifies noise. The optimal filter therefore approximately whitens the signal at low spatial frequencies and suppresses sufficiently high frequencies, with the cutoff determined by both noise levels and the power constraint.

This provides intuition for center--surround filtering. Opposing contributions from the center and surround attenuate slowly varying inputs, while the finite spatial extent of the center limits sensitivity to high frequencies. The resulting bandpass response is consistent with noise-limited whitening.

Dash-dotted: filtered signal power $C_s(k)|w(k)|^2$, approximately flat at low frequencies and attenuated at high frequencies. Dotted: input-noise power. The filter maximizes information subject to a unit variance constraint before output noise is added, without a temporal penalty. The output curve excludes noise; filter power gain and signal power have different units.}
    \label{fig:static-whitening}
\end{figure}

We now apply the same approach to drifting images, including the temporal smoothness constraint. We fix the noise at $\sigma_{in} = 0.2$ and $\sigma_{out} = 20$, similar to prior work~\cite{karklin2011efficient,roy2021inter,jun2021scene,jun2022efficient,belsten2025emergence}. Set $\eta=0.003$ which results in $D^* = 40$ arcmin${}^2$/s, the optimal filter is spatially bandpass for the illustrated noise conditions (Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}). The filter preferentially amplifies a range of spatial frequencies beyond the spectral crossover, where the stimulus power still falls as $k^{-4}$, while suppressing frequencies at which noise dominates. Using a spatially symmetric, temporally minimum-phase reconstruction (Appendix~\ref{sec:app_conversion}), the corresponding spatial kernel has a central lobe flanked by antagonistic surrounds.

This resolves the apparent conflict: drift whitens only part of the spectrum. The remaining correlations, together with noise and the output constraint, can still favor center--surround filtering. The whitening produced by movement therefore does not eliminate the role of bandpass neural filtering for optimal coding of drifting natural images.

\subsection{Joint adaptation of filters and drift}

Having established that center--surround filters remain favored at fixed drift, we next ask how the optimal diffusion constant and filters change with noise. For each pair of input and output noise levels, we search over $D$ and recompute the optimal filter subject to the same variance and smoothness constraint. The information rate has a maximum at a finite diffusion constant for the illustrated conditions (Fig.~\ref{fig:mi}, top left). Increasing input noise shifts this optimum toward larger diffusion constants, whereas increasing output noise shifts it toward smaller values (Fig.~\ref{fig:mi}, top right).

Allowing diffusion to adapt also changes the predicted response of the kernels to input noise. In the adaptive case, the spatial kernels broaden while retaining antagonistic surrounds, and the temporal kernels peak earlier as input noise increases (Fig.~\ref{fig:noise_kernels}, bottom left). With diffusion fixed, the surround weakens as input noise increases over the range shown (Fig.~\ref{fig:noise_kernels}, bottom right). These comparisons use the noise levels indicated in their respective legends. Joint adaptation of movement and filtering can therefore produce a different pattern of receptive-field changes from adaptation of the filter alone.

Optimal diffusion constants increase rapidly as a function of input noise, quickly exceeding a threshold that would be physically possible to move an eye. We haven't considered such contraints here. Weird and interesting prediction though that drift could increase information acquisition in low contrast or high noise conditions.

\subsection{Autocorrelation of moving images} \label{sec:app_autocorrelation}

Follow derivation process of Dong and Atick 1995 ~\cite{dong1995}. That is, get an equation that describes the spatio-temporal correlations $C_z(\xi,\tau)$ as a function of space $\xi$ and time $\tau$ lags. Then we can take the fourier transform in space and time. 
Define spatial-temporal autocorrelation function
\begin{align}
    C_z(\xi,\tau) &= \mathbb E_{x,t}\!\Big[ z(x,t)\,z(x+\xi,t+\tau) \Big] \\
           &= \mathbb E_{x,t}\!\Big[s\big(x-u(t)\big)\,s\big(x+\xi-u(t+\tau)\big) \Big].
\end{align}
We assume that $u(t)$ is governed by a stochastic process. For a temporal lag $\tau$, define the spatial displacement
\begin{equation}
    \Delta u(\tau) := u(t+\tau)-u(t),
\end{equation}
where the density of this displacement is given by $\Delta u(\tau) \sim \rho(\xi,\tau)$ and $\xi$ denotes a possible displacement value. Let $l = x - u(t)$ and substitute the stimulus $z(x,t)$ above with $s(x-u(t))$:
\begin{align}
    C_z(\xi,\tau) &= \mathbb E_{x,t}\!\Big[s\big(x-u(t)\big)\,s\big(x+\xi-u(t+\tau)\big) \Big] \\
        &= \mathbb E_{l,t}\Big[ s(l)\,s\big(l+\xi-\Delta u(\tau)\big)\Big]
\end{align}
Define the static image autocorrelation
\begin{align}
    C_s(\xi) := \mathbb E_{x}[s(x)s(x+\xi)].    
\end{align}
Assuming translation invariance, we can take expectation over brownian paths which then describes the spatiotemporal correlation, which can be equivalently described as spatial convolution
\begin{align}
    C_z(\xi,\tau) &= \mathbb E_{\Delta u(\tau)}\!\Big[C_s\big(\xi-\Delta u(\tau)\big)\Big] \\
           &=  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} C_s(\xi-\delta)\,\rho(\delta,\tau)\,d\delta\\
           &= (C_s*\rho(\cdot,\tau))(\xi).
\end{align}


\subsection{Power spectrum under Brownian drift} \label{sec:app_drift}

\begin{align}
    \rho(\xi,\tau) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi D|\tau|}} \exp\!\left( -\frac{\xi^2}{4D|\tau|} \right).
\end{align}
The spatial Fourier transform of Gaussian $\rho$ is another Gaussian:
\begin{align}
    \rho(k,\tau) = e^{-D|\tau|k^2}.
\end{align}
Putting these two together, you get
\begin{align}
    C_z(k,\tau;D)=C_s(k)\,e^{-D|\tau|k^2}
\end{align}

Now take the Fourier transform in time $\tau$:
\begin{align}
C_z(k,\omega;D) &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} C_z(k,\tau;D)e^{-i\omega \tau}\,d\tau \\
            &= C_s(k) \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-Dk^2|\tau|}e^{-i\omega \tau}\,d\tau.
\end{align}
The integral can be solved by splitting into two integrals from $(-\infty,0)$ and $[0,\infty)$ to deal with the $|t|$ in exponent which ends up working out into a simple form
\begin{align}
    \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-a|t|}e^{-i\omega t}\,dt = \frac{2a}{a^2+\omega^2}
\end{align}
with $a=Dk^2$, we get
\begin{align}
    C_z(k,\omega;D) = C_s(k)\,\frac{2Dk^2}{(Dk^2)^2+\omega^2}
\end{align}
up to a constant. Note that in the limit as $D$ goes to zero, all power lies at $\omega=0$, and within this plane the power is the input static image power
\begin{align}
    \lim_{D\rightarrow0^+}C_z(k,\omega;D) \propto C_s(k)\,\delta(\omega)
\end{align}


\subsection{Optimization of linear filters and diffusion} \label{sec:app_optimal_filters}

The squared magnitudes of the optimal linear filters $v(k,\omega) = |w(k,\omega)|^2$ can be solved for analytically in the Fourier domain.
The spatiotemporal response of the linear model is given by
\begin{align}
    r(k,\omega) = w(k,\omega) \big(z(k,\omega)+n_{in}(k,\omega) \big)+n_{out}(k,\omega).
\end{align}
We distinguish the space-time noise variances $\sigma_{in}^2$ and $\sigma_{out}^2$ from their power spectral densities. For Gaussain white noise, these densities are constant over the modeled frequency band and we denote their levels by $N_{in}$ and $N_{out}$. 
Under the Gaussian input approximation, the mutual information rate per unit space between the input $z$ and response $r$ is
\begin{align}
    I(Z;R) = \frac{1}{2} \int\frac{dk,d\omega}{(2\pi)^2}\, \log \left(1+ \frac{v(k,\omega) , C_z(k,\omega;D)}{N_{in}v(k,\omega) + N_{out}} \right).
\end{align}
The term in the integral has the familiar form of $\log\big(1+\text{SNR}(k,\omega)\big)$, which is then integrated over spatial and temporal frequencies. 

The combined power and temporal derivative constraint can also be written in terms of spatial and temporal frequencies as a function power spectrum of the filter response $C_y(k,\omega)$,
\begin{align}
    \int \frac{dk,d\omega}{(2\pi)^2} \left(1+\eta\omega^2\right) C_y(k,\omega)=1, \quad C_y(k,\omega) = v(k,\omega)\left(C_z(k,\omega;D)+N_{in}\right).
    \label{eqn:linear_fourier_constraint}
\end{align}
where $\omega^2$ arises from the temporal derivative constraint.

The information optimization objective subject to this constraint can be rewritten using a Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$. The Lagrangian for a given spatial and temporal frequency is
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}_{k,\omega} = \frac{1}{2} \log\left(1+\frac{v(k,\omega)C_z(k,\omega;D)}{N_{in}v(k,\omega)+N_{out}}\right) - \lambda(1+\eta\omega^2)v(k,\omega)\big(C_z(k,\omega;D)+N_{in}\big).
\end{align}
Setting $\partial\mathcal{L}{k,\omega}/\partial v=0$ at an active mode gives
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        &\frac{N_{out} C_z(k,\omega;D)} {2\big(N_{out}+(C_z(k,\omega;D)+N_{in})v(k,\omega)\big) \big(N_{out}+N_{in}v(k,\omega)\big)} \\
        &\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad = \lambda(1+\eta\omega^2) \big(C_z(k,\omega;D)+N_{in}\big).
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
For positive input and output noise levels, solving for $v(k,\omega)=|w(k,\omega)|^2$ yields
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        v(k,\omega) &= \frac{N_{out}}{N_{in}} \Bigg[ \frac{1}{2} \frac{C_z(k,\omega;D)}{C_z(k,\omega;D) + N_{in}} \\
        &\qquad {}\times \left( \sqrt{1 + \frac{N_{in}}{N_{out}} \frac{2}{\lambda(1+\eta\omega^2)C_z(k,\omega;D)}} + 1 \right) - 1 \Bigg]_+ .
    \end{split}
    \label{eqn:v_kw_optimal}
\end{equation}
% \begin{equation}
%     v(k,\omega) = \frac{N_{out}}{N_{in}} \Bigg[ \frac{1}{2} \frac{C_z(k,\omega;D)}{C_z(k,\omega;D) + N_{in}} \left( \sqrt{1 + \frac{N_{in}}{N_{out}} \frac{2}{\lambda(1+\eta\omega^2)C_z(k,\omega;D)}} + 1 \right) - 1 \Bigg]_+.
%     \label{eqn:v_kw_optimal}
% \end{equation}
The nonlinearity $[\cdot]_+ = \max(\cdot,0)$ ensures that filter powers $v(k,\omega)=|w(k,\omega)|^2$ are nonnegative. For each fixed diffusion constant $D$, we determine the Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$ numerically so that the optimal filter satisfies the combined variance and smoothness constraint in Eq.~\ref{eqn:linear_fourier_constraint}.

We find the optimal diffusion constant $D^*$ by numerically searching over $D$ to maximize the information rate, with the noise levels and $\eta$ held fixed. At each trial value of $D$, we update the stimulus spectrum $C_z(k,\omega;D)$ and recompute the optimal filter and its Lagrange multiplier so that the constraint remains satisfied.


At the reference noise levels, increasing the temporal penalty shifts the optimum toward smaller diffusion constants (Fig.~\ref{fig:diffusion-eta}). This dependence shows how the temporal constraint sets the preferred drift scale; its strength is held fixed when comparing different noise conditions in the main text.

\subsection{Reconstruction of space-time kernels} \label{sec:app_conversion}
We reconstruct the space--time kernel $w(x,t)$ by inverse Fourier transforming the optimal filter $w(k,\omega)=\sqrt{v(k,\omega)}\,e^{i\theta(k,\omega)}$:
\begin{align*}
    w(x,t) &=  \int\frac{dk,d\omega}{(2\pi)^2}\, w(k,\omega)\,e^{i(kx+\omega t)}.
\end{align*}
The optimization determines the filter amplitude but leaves its phase $\theta(k,\omega)$ unspecified. We choose a spatially symmetric,  emporally minimum-phase response. Since $v(k,\omega)$ is even in $k$, spatial symmetry is preserved by assigning the same phase to positive and negative spatial frequencies. The minimum temporal phase is given by the negative Hilbert transform of the log amplitude $\theta(k,\omega)= -\mathcal{H}_{\omega} \left\{\log\sqrt{v(k,\omega)}\right\}$.
